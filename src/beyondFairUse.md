# Introduction

Journalism is foundational to the functioning of healthy democracies. It supports civic engagement, reduces corruption, and combats misinformation. Yet today, journalism faces an existential crisis. Traffic — and the revenue it generates — has plummeted as internet activity consolidates around a handful of monopolistic platforms like Meta, Google, and TikTok ([Reuters Institute 2024](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/journalism-media-and-technology-trends-and-predictions-2024)). The rise of AI chatbots threatens to accelerate this disruption even further, shifting internet culture from a search-based to an answers-based model that bypasses publisher websites entirely.

This collapse in journalism’s economic viability has far-reaching consequences. It threatens democratic institutions by eroding the flow of trustworthy information, and it threatens AI development by undermining the production of one of its most valuable inputs: high-quality, factual, human-generated text. AI systems depend on journalistic content — not just during initial training, but also during fine-tuning and at inference time via retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — to function effectively. And while some AI companies have struck private licensing deals with major publishers, these are piecemeal, often exclusive, and reinforce already dangerous levels of concentration on both sides of the marketplace.

What’s needed is a more comprehensive, policy-driven response. Journalism must be treated as critical infrastructure for both democracy and innovation — a public good that cannot be sustained by market forces alone. This paper explores the economic pressures facing journalism today, the compounding threat posed by generative AI, and the limitations of current responses like copyright lawsuits and opt-out regimes. I argue that the incentives of news publishers, AI companies, and democratic governments are more aligned than they appear — and that effective governance mechanisms can support both the vitality of journalism and the continued progress of artificial intelligence.

# Section 1 — journalism and democracy

The stakes of maintaining journalism's viability are high — journalism is foundational to the functioning of healthy democracies. In a research review conducted by the Democracy Fund, it was found that strong news media (especially local news) positively impacts civic engagement and voter turnout, especially for local elections, in democracies around the world. Local news reporting also helped reduce corruption at the governmental and corporate levels and reduced governmental inefficiencies. According to the Democracy Fund, "Every dollar spent on local news produces hundreds of dollars in public benefit by exposing corruption & keeping an eye on government spending" ([Stearns 2022](https://democracyfund.org/idea/how-we-know-journalism-is-good-for-democracy/)). Incumbent politicians in communities without local news are also more likely to remain in power in an election.

At the level of information exchange, journalism is vital in combating misinformation, propaganda, and knowledge silos. Local news is again important — communities with strong local news have greater social cohesion and less division ([Yamamoto 2011](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/073953291103200103)). Misinformation campaigns and propaganda rely on distrust in existing institutions and media outlets, which can be counteracted with strong journalistic traditions alongside social factors like public education. Research shows that disinformation flourishes in environments with weak press freedom and declining trust in mainstream media ([Menaker 2022](https://pen.org/report/hard-news-journalists-and-the-threat-of-disinformation)). As we have seen the decline in the economic viability of journalism in many democracies across the world, we have seen a correlative increase in political polarization, misinformation, and knowledge silos ([Newman et al. 2024](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/RISJ_DNR_2024_Digital_v10%20lr.pdf)). These increases lead to greater governmental corruption, radicalization, populism, and autocracy. The second Trump presidency's increase in executive power and erosion of civic liberties speaks to this decline in democratic well-being ([Eisen et al. 2024](https://www.justsecurity.org/92714/american-autocracy-threat-tracker/)). While this can't all be attributed to a decline in the strength of journalism in the United States and other democracies, there is historical precedence that it is a contributing factor. For democracy to remain resilient, journalism's decline must be counteracted.

# Section 2 — journalism is vital to AI development

Journalism, in particular the high-quality text and media content that is produced by publishers, is a valuable resource for AI companies. In fact, it's one of the most important data inputs to train AI models, and it's a critical resource for them to be effective as chatbots as it facilitates retrieval augmented generation (RAG) for up-to-date information. The dependency on content from journalists isn't easily replaced, and any decline in journalism could result in it becoming harder to train more advanced AI systems. This creates an incentive for AI companies to both gain access to journalistic content (by legitimate or illicit means) and to ensure the stream of new up-to-date content remains flowing. While this dependency has risks given the state of journalism, it also aligns incentives between AI companies and news publishers if the relationship is managed correctly.

### AI training is dependent on journalism

Courtney Radsch's excellent research shows how dependent AI models are on journalism and news publishers for high-quality data ([2024](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)). This dependency includes all phases of AI, from base model training to fine-tuning to applications. In base model training, news publishers make up a large portion of the training data, especially the high-quality training data — comprising "half of the top 10 sites" in a commonly used dataset used by both Google and Meta to train models ([Radsch 2024 3](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)). The New York Times emphasizes this point in their lawsuit against Microsoft and OpenAI, showing how two prominent datasets, WebText2 and Common Crawl, both rely extensively on news publications (*[The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation and OpenAI, Inc.](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/new-york-times-microsoft-open-ai-complaint.pdf)*[ 2023, 26](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/new-york-times-microsoft-open-ai-complaint.pdf)). To illustrate, below is the breakdown of token sources in Common Crawl (note that the scale is logarithmic). The New York Times is third after Google Patents and Wikipedia, followed by the LA Times and the Guardian. Forbes, Huffington Post, the Washington Post, and others aren't far behind.

![Token counts per website in the Common Crawl 4 dataset, on a logarithmic scale.](/images/beyond-fair-use/tokensperwebsitecommoncrawl4.png)
Tokens per source in the Common Crawl 4 dataset. Data source: [Dodge et al 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08758).

The proprietary and secretive nature of many AI datasets makes it difficult to determine with certainty the makeup of journalistic sources in the training data for ChatGPT, Claude, and others, but the evidence in the New York Times finding is compelling and suggests that it is substantial. Given that, without these datasets, it's unlikely that AI chatbots would have achieved their current capability levels. Future improvements in AI will require even more high-quality data — as we attempt to scale up the number of parameters in an LLM, we need to match this increase with an increase in data to get full use of the parameter count ([Hoffmann et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15556)). Epoch AI shows that language model datasets have been increasing by 3.5 times per year since 2010 ([Rahman and Owen 2024](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/dataset-size-trend)). The quality of the data matters here as well — models trained on smaller datasets of high-quality data can outperform models trained on larger datasets of lower quality text. As Penedo et al show, models trained on their 38-billion-token dataset, FineWeb-Edu, outperform models trained on a larger, less curated 300-billion-token dataset across benchmarks ([Penedo et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17557), p.9). The search for additional sources of high-quality data has led to concern in the industry. Researchers are concerned we’ll run out of data in the near future ([Villalobos et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04325)), while OpenAI itself has emphasized the importance of having access to copyright-protected works in order to continue to release competitive models, claiming "If the PRC's developers have unfettered access to data and American companies are left without fair use access, the race for AI is effectively over" ([OpenAI 2025, 11](https://cdn.openai.com/global-affairs/ostp-rfi/ec680b75-d539-4653-b297-8bcf6e5f7686/openai-response-ostp-nsf-rfi-notice-request-for-information-on-the-development-of-an-artificial-intelligence-ai-action-plan.pdf)). While there is ongoing research into the use of synthetic (i.e. AI generated) data for training purposes, this is currently experimental and it's yet to be shown that it will be possible to improve models by relying on this kind of data ([Villalobos et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04325)). The urgency in OpenAI's lobbying efforts to American lawmakers suggests that this is far from a sure bet. Fine-tuning datasets may be particularly difficult to replace as they’re especially vital in shaping the quality and tone of the model’s output. As things stand today, for AI to continue to improve, journalists need to continue to produce text for it to train on.

### RAG and modern chatbots

Beyond training, journalism is vital for deployed models to be useful, especially when referencing current events. This is because all modern AI chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, etc.) incorporate an internet search feature, allowing them to search the internet for relevant information before responding. This improves the accuracy of its response, especially when referencing current events that have occurred after the knowledge cutoff date of the trained model. Radsch has gone to lengths to demonstrate how important news and journalism is for this to work effectively: "Perplexity AI, for example, prioritizes what founder Aravind Srinivas calls 'peer-reviewed domains' such as leading journalism outlets because it is high-quality, has been through an editing process and includes background research and source verification" ([Radsch 2024, 4](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)). In an environment where more content is being produced in general because of the rise of AI content generation, we should expect this weighting to become even more prominent to ensure that information provided by the chatbot is accurate, as failure to do so will reduce the competitiveness of the chatbot as a consumer tool. And unlike the case of training data, it's difficult to theorize a way to circumvent this requirement with synthetic data — without journalists reporting on current events, chatbots themselves will be fundamentally unable to provide up-to-date information.

While current arguments by AI companies in favor of fair use copyright policies are being used to lobby American lawmakers to the detriment of the publishing industry, they also indicate that AI companies have a vested interest in the continued existence of news publishers as a source of data, both for training and for RAG. This is far from the state of affairs with social media platforms, which "have little incentive to subsidize the wages of the journalists whose work these platforms distribute" according to Matt Pearce, journalist and Co-Director of the USC Synthetic News Working Group ([2024](https://mattdpearce.substack.com/p/news-california-journalism-preservation)). This alignment of incentives opens an opportunity for governance structures that benefit both sides, if they are well-implemented. I'll discuss potential avenues to achieve this in the final section.

# Section 3 — journalism's current challenge

Despite the importance of journalism for democratic structures, in the United States and abroad, journalism is facing a crisis of economic viability. Publishers are closing their doors and laying off journalists. A large contributing factor to this decline is the loss of ad revenue and online subscriptions as internet traffic consolidates into a few monopolistic platforms (Meta, Google, TikTok) that receive the majority of traffic. This is set to worsen with AI chatbots moving us from a search-based internet to an answers-based internet culture. This presents an existential threat to the viability of journalism, especially local news and investigative reporting.

Journalism's decline has been well-documented. In 2023 alone, 3,000 journalists in the United States were laid off ([Radsch 2024, 1](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)), even as the unemployment rate reached pre-pandemic levels ([Bureau of Labor Statistics n.d.](https://www.bls.gov/charts/employment-situation/civilian-unemployment-rate.htm)). This continued a strong trend observed since 2005 ([Schmemann 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/11/26/opinion/local-newspapers-democracy-journalism.html)).

There has also been a decline in local US newspapers (including digital publications). Data source: [The State of Local News | Local News Initiative](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2024/report/)

Similar declines have been observed around the world, according to the Reuters Institute Digital News Report, with “layoffs, closures, and other cuts due to a combination of rising costs, falling advertising revenues, and sharp declines in traffic from social media” ([Newman et al. 2024](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2024/dnr-executive-summary)). Pew Research has shown that, while revenue is decreasing across the board, the greatest decline has been caused by decreases in advertising revenue.

![U.S. newspaper advertising and circulation revenue from 2013 to 2020.](/images/beyond-fair-use/newspaperadrevenuebyyear.png)
Data source: [Local Newspapers Fact Sheet | Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-newspapers/)

The decrease in advertisement revenue has been tied to the rise of monopolistic platforms like Google Search, Meta (Facebook and Instagram), and TikTok consolidating internet traffic. People get more information (including news) directly on these platforms, decreasing hyperlink traffic and thus decreasing traffic to the sites *producing* the information that is shared on these same platforms. Google's ad revenue over the same period of journalistic decline has skyrocketed. Research from Columbia University estimates that $10 to $12 billion of Google's annual ad revenue is from news publisher sources ([Schiffirn et al. 2023](https://s3.documentcloud.org/documents/24129986/paying-for-news.pdf)).

![Google advertising revenue by year from 2000 to 2024.](/images/beyond-fair-use/googleadrevenuebyyear.png)
Data source: [Google: advertising revenue 2024 | Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/266249/advertising-revenue-of-google/)

Why is this happening? The digitization of news initially led to more traffic. An exchange was made between digital platforms like Google and Facebook that they would provide links to sources, and, in turn, direct web traffic to these sources, in exchange for crawling these websites for data. This implicit agreement allowed media publishers to sell ad space on their websites or charge subscriptions, and thus profit from the exchange. However, the internet no longer works in this way. The hyperlink is dying, with studies showing decreased “traffic referrals from Facebook to publishers by 48% last year and from X by 27%” ([Newman et al. 2024, 12](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/RISJ_DNR_2024_Digital_v10%20lr.pdf)). We are approaching what many call the "Google Zero" moment — when information is accessed only on a few monopolistic platforms without redirecting traffic. Whether this moment actually arrives or not is unclear, but what is clear is that web traffic and ad revenue is consolidating, and a commensurate decrease in ad revenue for news publishers is occurring. This presents an existential threat to the economic viability of journalism and, by extension, the stability of democracies around the world.

# Section 4 — new pressures from AI

Content production, human-computer interaction, and the internet itself are all being reshaped by the advent of AI, in particular LLMs and AI chatbots. While some argue there are advantages and opportunities for journalists and publishers ([Caswell 2023](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/ai-and-journalism-whats-next)), these new technologies are likely to exacerbate the issues already faced in the industry. There are two main factors that contribute to this exacerbation — the increased proliferation of online content, especially text-based content facilitated by LLMs, and the further consolidation of digital platforms around AI chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude. If there isn't a governance response to address these issues, it could be the death knell for the economic viability of journalism, with devastating consequences both for democracy and the progress of AI models.

### More content and competition

The decrease in the cost of producing content, in particular text, can be expected to increase the amount of total content being published and the amount of publishers in the industry. LLMs make it much cheaper to produce large volumes of text. The Standford Artificial Intelligence Index report shows that “The most frequent application [of AI] is generating initial drafts of text documents (9%)” ([Maslej et al. 2024, 266](https://hai-production.s3.amazonaws.com/files/hai_ai-index-report-2024-smaller2.pdf)) and that “users completed tasks in 26% to 73% less time than their counterparts without AI access” ([Maslej et al. 2024, 273](https://hai-production.s3.amazonaws.com/files/hai_ai-index-report-2024-smaller2.pdf)). While the quality of the text that LLMs are capable of producing is subjective and up for debate, it has certainly improved dramatically over the past five years since GPT-3 was released, and we can expect it to generally improve over the near-term. There are two impacts of this increase in content that are particularly relevant to journalism's economic viability — content fatigue for consumers, and price pressures on producers.

As the cost of creating content decreases from increasingly affordable writing tools powered by LLMs, there will be an incentive for more publishers to enter the marketplace, according to basic economic principles of supply-side cost changes. Additionally, existing publications will be incentivized to publish more content, and to use AI tools to try to ensure that content is as targeted as possible. While this offers some opportunities for news publishers to reduce costs and experiment with new formats, these efficiencies are available to all market participants, eliminating any sustainable competitive advantage.

Content fatigue is a phenomenon whereby consumers, overwhelmed with the quantity of content, disengage entirely. In the past ten years, across democratic countries, we've seen a disengagement with news media ([Newman et al. 2024](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/RISJ_DNR_2024_Digital_v10%20lr.pdf)). While the exact reasons for this trend are multifold, research from the Reuters Institute Digital News Report suggests that information overload is a significant factor across democratic countries, with 39% of respondents saying they feel “worn out” by the amount of news, citing this as a reason for news avoidance ([Newman et al. 2024, 28](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2024-06/RISJ_DNR_2024_Digital_v10%20lr.pdf)).

If this is true, the influx of new content production enabled by lowered costs via LLMs will lead to a paradoxical effect — there will be lower overall readership. This means that not only will there be more competition for readership with new emerging AI-centric publishers, but there will be a smaller overall readership over which they'll be competing. This additional noise and complexity in the marketplace may also increase the value of brand name recognition, bolstering major outlets like the New York Times ([Radsch 2024](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)), but making it more difficult for new ventures.

In addition to content fatigue potentially contributing to lower readership, increased content will also increase competition for ad revenue. With the proliferation of content platforms, the price that advertisers are willing to pay to post on any one platform will go down — there is simply too much competition. Here again major outlets may be spared, depending on the resilience of their readership in the face of increased competition, but they will face price pressure and it will be increasingly difficult for new publishers to appear in the marketplace. We could see a few large publishers capturing the majority of ad revenue in the space, while the rest of the marketplace fights over scraps.

### Less clickthrough/more consolidation of web traffic

With the advent of AI chatbots like Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and more, the overall revenue from ads for publishers is likely to decrease because web traffic referral will dissipate. Traditional search is rapidly being replaced by AI-generated summaries and "answer engines," with market research indicating that 25% of search queries may shift to AI interfaces by 2026 ([Gartner, 2024](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2024-02-19-gartner-predicts-search-engine-volume-will-drop-25-percent-by-2026-due-to-ai-chatbots-and-other-virtual-agents)). Rather than increasing traffic towards publisher websites, however, this is accelerating the death of the hyperlink-based internet as users can remain on the site hosting the chatbot and get all the information needed. If users stay on these platforms, there is little reason for advertisers to purchase ad space on the sites of the publishers. Additionally, there is less motivation for consumers to pay for subscriptions as they're able to access the information they need for free, often directly from the paywalled source. While these chatbots provide links to the sources of information, the primary reason to go to those sites is removed as the information is already provided. Some users may decide to go to those sources regardless, either to check the accuracy of the chatbot's summary or to learn more, but the overall flow of web traffic will continue to be reduced. Initial data is not encouraging — while the clickthrough rate for no-ad sources on Google was about 40% in 2024 ([First Page Sage 2024](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/clickthrough-rates-ctrs-by-industry/)), AI chatbot startup [Miso.ai](http://Miso.ai) reports that clickthrough rates on their platform is just 10% to 15% ([Radsch 2024](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/664f737f1986625f77319d19/1716482943712/Value+of+Journalism+to+AI-Radsch-CJL-OMI.pdf)). Forbes reports that ChatGPT and Perplexity reduce clickthrough rates by a drastic 96% compared to Google Search ([Shrivastava 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rashishrivastava/2025/03/03/openai-perplexity-ai-search-traffic-report/)). If these metrics are accurate, even if AI models get only slightly better with time, the economic viability of journalism will be under even more pressure, and democracies across the world will be weakened.

# Section 5 — current approaches to address the issue

This danger hasn't gone unnoticed, spurring several stakeholders to act. Current initiatives to protect journalism, including the NYT lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft and the EU AI Act's opt-out structure, often center on copyright restrictions, making the debate one about copyright fair use/dealing. However, should these lawsuits be successful, restricting AI labs from training their models on journalistic sources, it will cause profound issues for AI labs seeking to improve their models — opening the door for AI models developed in countries that don't respect international intellectual property law, like China, Russia, and other autocratic nations.

### Market negotiations

In the absence of clarity on whether AI model training constitutes copyright fair use/dealing, several groups are pursuing resolutions to the issue. In particular, OpenAI has pursued several licensing deals with publishers for access to their data.  OpenAI has struck deals with major outlets like News Corp, Axel Springer, Condé Nast, and others ([Ghaffary 2024](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-08-20/conde-nast-openai-strike-multiyear-partnership-in-new-ai-deal)). This is beneficial in that it provides OpenAI with the data they need to continue to develop and deploy their models while remunerating news publishers for the use of their content. Because it goes through the market, it circumvents some inefficiency that legislative solutions may impose.

However, inefficiencies remain, especially in cases where the publisher and the AI company are unable to come to an agreement. This is the case with the New York Times — they entered into negotiations with OpenAI and Microsoft but were unable to come to an agreement. A lawsuit followed wherein the Times argues that OpenAI and Microsoft violated their copyright. This has turned into a protracted legal battle, drawing resources from all parties. It is only one example of an outstanding lawsuit.

In addition to being inefficient, these types of arrangements risk increasing market concentration, reducing overall competition in the AI space and entrenching the position of major groups like OpenAI and Anthropic. Because new businesses entering the AI industry will need to negotiate agreements with publishers to train and use models on an equal footing with the likes of OpenAI and Anthropic, the bar to entry in the market will be much higher, contributing to further consolidation of the market into a few large companies. OpenAI itself has emphasized this difficultly as part of its effort to convince the US government to resolve the fair use debate in their favor. They write in their National Science Foundation report that, in regions that enforce copyright protections, "access to important AI inputs is less predictable" and "Unpredictable availability of inputs hinders AI innovation, particularly for smaller, newer entrants with limited budgets" ([OpenAI 2025](https://cdn.openai.com/global-affairs/ostp-rfi/ec680b75-d539-4653-b297-8bcf6e5f7686/openai-response-ostp-nsf-rfi-notice-request-for-information-on-the-development-of-an-artificial-intelligence-ai-action-plan.pdf), 10). While this argument is certainly motivated by OpenAI’s interests, there is validity to their argument. The team at DeepSeek has shown that competitive models can be developed by smaller teams, but the quality of data used is a key determinant ([DeepSeek-AI et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02954)). Democratic countries that respect copyright laws risk falling behind if claims of copyright fair use are denied in the courts and individually negotiated licensing deals are the only way forward.

Small news publishers will also struggle because they can't negotiate on equal terms with large AI companies. There has been a general consolidation of the publisher space into larger conglomerates ([Abernathy 2023](https://localnewsinitiative.northwestern.edu/projects/state-of-local-news/2023/report/)), which has been problematic in its own right for journalism. A situation where smaller publishers aren't given the means to deal with AI companies (for example, via collective bargaining) risks exacerbating this market concentration because they'll be unable to get revenue for their data. And even for larger publishers like the New York Times, they're in an unequal bargaining position with tech companies because the marketplace for companies creating advanced AI is already so concentrated. This puts them at a disadvantage, as seen in their dealings with Google ([Pearce 2024](https://mattdpearce.substack.com/p/news-california-journalism-preservation)). From both sides of the bargaining table, large companies are better equipped to navigate this market, to the detriment of competition and small businesses.

### Opt-out structure of the EU AI Act

Unlike in countries like Canada and the US where it's unclear if publishers can legally deny AI companies the right to use their data for training purposes, the EU AI Act specifically provides options for publishers to opt out of training using robots.txt — a common file that tells bots what pages they don't have permission to scrape. However, this path presents its own challenges, both for model use and for competitiveness of AI companies in democratic nations.

Allowing publishers to opt-out of data scraping gives them the ability to license or sell their data, providing a revenue path for publishers. However, because there is no structure that covers model use — for example, RAG — news publishers still risk having their revenue drop dramatically given the decline in clickthrough events we're seeing in users of AI chatbots. While it's likely that fine-tuning falls under Article 4(3) of the EU AI Act, RAG operates fundamentally differently, in a manner much like a traditional search engine. All modern AI chatbots use RAG to improve the quality of their results, ensuring they are accurate and up to date. However, as we've seen, the overall number of click-through events for chatbots appears to be far lower than traditional search engines, which means the publishers aren't being remunerated for providing the basis for this improved product quality. The EU AI Act gives companies no simple way to navigate the challenges this will present.

The EU AI Act blocks AI companies from using publisher data if they opt out via robots.txt, but this means that AI companies in Europe or other nations that respect copyright laws risk losing access to large quantities of high-quality training data without engaging in extensive negotiation processes. This puts them at a disadvantage to companies in countries like China, where European copyright and intellectual property laws are routinely violated, as documented by the U.S. Trade Representative's annual Special 301 Report ([Office of the United States Trade Representative 2024](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/2024%20Special%20301%20Report.pdf)). OpenAI has voiced this concern, lobbying US lawmakers in an attempt to prevent similar legislation in the US. In particular, OpenAI claims that copyright fair use "is even more critical to continued American leadership on AI in the wake of recent events in the PRC" ([OpenAI 2025, 10](https://cdn.openai.com/global-affairs/ostp-rfi/ec680b75-d539-4653-b297-8bcf6e5f7686/openai-response-ostp-nsf-rfi-notice-request-for-information-on-the-development-of-an-artificial-intelligence-ai-action-plan.pdf)). The success of much smaller models capable of competing with OpenAI and other major AI firms coming out of China, like DeepSeek, appears to affirm this concern — there is little, if any, gap in capabilities between AI models developed by democratic nations and those developed in China. OpenAI claims that DeepSeek violated the terms of their API, using output from ChatGPT to train their smaller models (a process known as distillation), indicating already that there may be unfair practices at play in the AI race ([Metz 2025](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/01/29/technology/openai-deepseek-data-harvest.html)). And while some of the fears around Chinese dominance in AI appear to be based in xenophobia and nationalism ([McInerney 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/20539517241227873)), given the potential for AI to shape the information landscape and warfare, ceding dominance of the field to any autocratic nation represents a legitimate strategic concern for democratic societies.

# Section 6 — three models for journalism's future

Fortunately, the threat faced to both democracy and AI development aligns incentives in such a way that there is a beneficial solution to resolve the issue — a win-win scenario for journalism, democracy, and innovation. Finding this path forward will require a rethinking of priorities from multiple stakeholders involved — government, AI companies, and news publishers. I propose three different approaches to resolving the issue and weigh their respective merits — one that relies on the market to exchange content as data, one that legislates negotiation structures between news publishers and tech companies, and one that creates a public fund to subsidize journalism. All approaches improve the current situation, and each have their own drawbacks. While the third and final option — creating a public fund — is my preferred solution when considered in isolation, it’s likely that a combination of these approaches would be optimal.

### AI content marketplace

The first approach involves creating a content marketplace for news media exchange with AI companies. This approach requires courts to reject AI companies' claims that large-scale ingestion of news content constitutes fair use/dealing, establishing an economic incentive for AI firms to pay for access to journalistic content. To avoid the challenges identified in the previous section, there will need to rapidly follow a marketplace where publishers, especially smaller companies, can sell their data to AI companies. This approach benefits from requiring little government intervention. It also can build on existing business models, like those being developed by Tollbit, ProRata, ScalePost, and others ([Shrivastava 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rashishrivastava/2024/12/23/these-startups-are-making-sure-ai-companies-pay-up-for-taking-content/)). In fact, should courts around the world follow the EU AI Act and rule in favor of copyright holders, it is likely that this marketplace will occur organically.

However, the marketplace approach depends entirely on copyright rulings favorable to publishers and risks fragmenting licensing rights internationally. Should the US deem AI data mining an example of fair use, for example, AI companies will have little incentive to participate in a data marketplace in the EU, Canada, or elsewhere — especially given they can still use data from these regions in search-powered results. An additional issue is that this marketplace structure incentivizes only a particular kind of journalism — that which produces high volumes of well-written text. Incentives for publishers to invest in (notoriously expensive) investigative reporting and civic-focused content will be relatively lower, to the detriment of the health of democratic institutions. Perhaps most concerning, there will be no barriers to major firms like OpenAI and News Corp striking exclusive licensing deals, weakening the market and disadvantaging smaller players on both sides.

Because of these factors, for this approach to be successful, it will need to be combined with other stakeholder initiatives. At the international level, there will need to be some cooperation on copyright legislation so that AI companies can’t opt-out of the marketplace by sourcing all their data from a region that has determined AI training constitutes fair use/dealing. To encourage civically beneficial journalism like investigative reporting, governments and philanthropic institutions may need to provide financial incentives, like grants and tax breaks. AI companies can support here too, helping journalists leverage technology for their reporting and contributing funding for AI-powered initiatives, like the New York Times reporting on weapons use by Israel in Gaza ([Stein et al. 2023](https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/21/world/middleeast/israel-gaza-bomb-investigation.html)). Finally, strong anti-trust laws will be beneficial in preventing larger companies from weakening the competitive marketplace.

### Legislation supporting negotiating between publishers and AI companies

The second approach builds on existing legislative frameworks established in Australia and Canada, expanding them to address AI-specific challenges. Canada's Bill C-18 and Australia's News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code both create frameworks enabling news publishers to negotiate with digital platforms that redistribute their work. Digital platforms are required to participate in negotiations, with fallback to government arbitration should negotiations fail. These bills recognize that digital platforms profit from sharing news media while the benefits for publishers have diminished for reasons discussed earlier in this paper. Similar legislation is currently being considered at the federal level in the US ([U.S. Congress 2023](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/1094/text)).

These legislative initiatives currently don't cover AI's use of journalism. To safeguard journalism and democracy, critical amendments must extend coverage to AI companies' use of news publisher content. This legislation operates independently from copyright fair use/dealing rulings, focusing instead on the value that content created by news publishers provides to digital platforms.
The most straightforward extension would cover retrieval augmented generation (RAG), which closely resembles search functionality — chatbots providing information by drawing from web pages. In this case, negotiations would address how much value digital platforms derive from these results. Given that most platforms don't currently generate revenue from advertisements in their AI products, this value may initially be modest. Extending legislation to cover training data presents more complexity as it differs fundamentally from search. However, quantitative assessments of training data using news publication content, like that conducted by the New York Times as part of their lawsuit, offer a promising framework, particularly when weighted to reflect the relatively high quality of journalistic content (*[The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation and OpenAI, Inc.](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/new-york-times-microsoft-open-ai-complaint.pdf)*[ 2023](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/new-york-times-microsoft-open-ai-complaint.pdf)).

If properly adapted, these initiatives represent a promising path forward. They would build upon demonstrated interest from both AI companies and news publishers in negotiating deals, as evidenced by OpenAI's agreements with several outlets. Rather than continuing with piecemeal arrangements, a legal framework would establish clear expectations for these negotiations and provide arbitration mechanisms should talks fail — avoiding situations like the lawsuit that followed failed negotiations between the New York Times and OpenAI. Furthermore, by providing collective bargaining rights for news publishers, this approach would help prevent scenarios where only large publications benefit from negotiations. Exemptions for smaller digital platforms, as included in proposed US federal legislation and in Canadian and Australian laws, would allow new market entrants to compete fairly.

This approach carries risks, and similar bills have shown mixed results. The primary concern is that companies might opt out of negotiations entirely. In Australia, while Google negotiated agreements with news agencies and now maintains over 80 such arrangements, Meta has refused further negotiations ([Australian Treasury 2022](https://treasury.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-11/p2022-343549.pdf)). This prompted the Australian government to consider legislation imposing taxes as a disincentive for opting out ([McGuirk 2024](https://apnews.com/article/digital-platforms-australia-tax-news-media-db252d95d6ee641dc3ccf1df4af4e573)). In Canada, Google settled with the federal government for an annual fee of $100 million CAD, while Meta chose to stop providing news links on their platform in Canada altogether — a decision that has harmed Canadian publishers and readers more than Meta itself ([Crabbe-Field et al. 2025](https://www.mediatechdemocracy.com/c-18-news-bargaining-report)). These outcomes demonstrate that smaller markets like Australia and Canada remain vulnerable to tech companies abandoning their markets entirely. For these initiatives to succeed, larger markets — either the EU or US, but ideally both — must establish similar legislation. Without participation from these major markets, such initiatives could potentially create more harm than good for journalism.

Critics like Rasmus Nielsen note that this legislation effectively transfers wealth between private companies without necessarily advancing broader democratic goals ([Patja et al. 2021](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/lawfare-podcast-rasmus-kleis-nielsen-australia-facebook-and-future-journalism)). Smaller local publications and those serving underrepresented communities may still struggle to negotiate effectively, as their content often isn't optimized for maximum engagement on digital platforms or maximum effectiveness as training data. Additionally, Nielsen argues that by mandating wealth transfer from dominant tech platforms, an unintended dynamic may emerge: publishers become incentivized to favor these platforms to maximize their negotiating leverage, potentially increasing their dependence on the very monopolistic entities they're trying to counterbalance ([Patja et al. 2021](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/lawfare-podcast-rasmus-kleis-nielsen-australia-facebook-and-future-journalism)).

Despite these concerns, the models established by Bill C-18 and Australia's News Media and Digital Platforms Mandatory Bargaining Code provide the most pragmatic legislative path to addressing the impending crisis in journalism that AI is likely to accelerate. Governments worldwide now have both a model and data on the consequences of this approach. Given the threat to both democracy and AI innovation that would result from journalism's economic collapse, it's vital that this legislation be broadened and adopted by more democratic countries, especially those with larger markets that have greater influence over technology companies.

### Public subsidies for journalism

The third approach treats journalism's decline as a market failure requiring public intervention through a dedicated funding mechanism. This approach directly addresses the core issues — threats to democratic institutions and continued AI innovation caused by the collapse of journalism. Creating a public fund by taxing or extracting fees from tech companies that use news publishers' content can be combined with affirming copyright fair use/dealing. This ensures journalism remains viable through subsidization while allowing AI companies in democratic countries to continue innovating and competing internationally. Unlike collective bargaining agreements that explicitly favor news media over technology, this solution can benefit all stakeholders. California's proposed SB 1327 — which would impose a 7.25% tax on large companies extracting data from news publishers — exemplifies this approach ([California Legislature 2024](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB1327/id/2992038)). Unlike exclusive licensing deals or collective bargaining, this solution aims to benefit both journalism and technological innovation simultaneously.

The strengths of this public fund approach are substantial. First, it directly addresses journalism's financial crisis without hampering AI development, allowing both sectors to thrive. OpenAI's willingness to enter licensing deals and Google's payments into public funds in California and Canada demonstrate potential feasibility. Second, by focusing on data mining rather than link publication, this framework prevents companies from evading regulation by banning news content, as Matt Pearce ([2024](https://mattdpearce.substack.com/p/california-unveils-a-second-major)) notes: "a levy on users' implicit barter of their private information… does not appear to be reliant on the precise nature to which platforms choose to crawl, ingest, display or synthesize our journalism… perhaps decreasing the ability of platforms to attempt regulatory evasion." Third, when combined with provisions that clarify AI training as fair use/dealing, this solution aligns the interests of both industries — journalism receives sustainable funding while AI companies gain access to training data worth far more than the imposed tax ([Kandpal and Raffel 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.12427)). Finally, this approach recognizes journalism's fundamental value beyond its copyright status, supporting Pearce's ([2024](https://mattdpearce.substack.com/p/california-unveils-a-second-major)) argument that "copyright and paywalls are simply means to an end, and that end is the pursuit of human knowledge and self-liberation."

However, this approach carries significant risks. Most concerning is potential government overreach, as public subsidies give governments control over journalism's funding, threatening editorial independence and free speech. Under hostile administrations, non-compliant news organizations could face defunding. We’re seeing this now in the United States, as the Trump administration is threatening to defund PBS and NPR for allegedly spreading "radical, woke propaganda disguised as 'news'" ([The White House 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/articles/2025/04/the-npr-pbs-grift-has-ripped-us-off-for-too-long/)). Additionally, creating new taxation frameworks requires careful implementation to avoid unintended consequences for smaller tech companies or international competitiveness.

The public fund model, if adopted with appropriate safeguards against governmental interference, offers the most promising path forward. It transforms news content usage into something analogous to utility extraction, where companies pay for access to a public good beyond certain usage thresholds. Further research should examine how countries with established public funding traditions — like Norway, Switzerland, and Germany — successfully maintain editorial independence and how these lessons might apply to tech-funded journalism subsidies. This balanced approach could provide stable funding for journalism while allowing AI systems to flourish on a reliable foundation of high-quality data, ultimately preserving both democratic discourse and technological innovation.

# Conclusion — preserving journalism is critical for both democracy and AI

The technological and economic disruptions to journalism present an existential challenge that demands an urgent, coordinated response. If nothing is done, the continued decline of journalism will have far-reaching consequences — not only undermining democracy by eroding the flow of trustworthy information but also threatening AI development by compromising one of its most valuable inputs: high-quality, factual, human-generated text.

The proposed paths forward each offer potential benefits for journalism and AI development, but the public fund model provides the strongest framework for aligning the incentives of all stakeholders while supporting the civic function of journalism in democratic societies. By treating high-quality journalism as a public good deserving of structural support — rather than merely a commercial product — we can ensure both the continued vitality of independent journalism and the responsible advancement of AI technology.

However, substantial questions remain that require further investigation. First, what companies should contribute to such public funds? While AI companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google are obvious candidates, what about companies like Nvidia that profit from AI without directly using journalistic content, or companies like Apple and Amazon that are integrating AI into their products and services? Clear criteria must be established to determine which entities benefit from journalism's contributions to AI development.

Second, determining appropriate funding levels presents significant challenges. What percentage of revenue derived from AI applications should be directed toward journalism? How do we quantify the value of journalistic content in training and operating AI systems? Economic research to establish these parameters will be crucial for creating fair and sustainable funding mechanisms.

Third, the approaches outlined in this paper need not be mutually exclusive. A hybrid solution might combine elements of all three—maintaining a marketplace for high-value content licensing, establishing collective bargaining frameworks for news publishers, and creating public funds to support journalism's civic function. Finding the optimal combination requires careful consideration of market dynamics and policy objectives.

Finally, ongoing evaluation of existing legislative efforts is essential. The experiences of Australia, Canada, and the EU provide valuable data about what works and what doesn't when regulating the relationship between technology platforms and news publishers. As more jurisdictions implement similar measures, comparative analysis can inform more effective policy design.

The crisis facing journalism demands urgent action, but the alignment of incentives between AI companies and news publishers offers hope for constructive solutions. By recognizing journalism as critical infrastructure for both democracy and technological innovation, we can craft governance mechanisms that sustain journalism's vital role while enabling continued AI advancement. The task ahead is complex, requiring cooperation between governments, technology companies, and publishers. If successfully accomplished, we not only preserve the raw material that powers cutting-edge AI, but also safeguard the institutional backbone of democracy.
