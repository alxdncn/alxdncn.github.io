Today I’ll be reading Kitty Horrorshow’s *ANATOMY* as an ecofeminist, queer ecological videogame. I argue that the digital space it presents is depicted in a manner subversive to “environmental storytelling” perspectives that postulate the relationship between players and digital spaces as that of a stable subject enacting their will upon and extracting knowledge from a passive background environment.

Before speaking directly about *ANATOMY*, I’d like to discuss the concept of environmental storytelling, in particular Henry Jenkins’s famous articulation of the concept in his 2004 essay “Game Design as Narrative Architecture.” Environmental storytelling has become an influential concept in game design and criticism, being used in countless fan accounts of games while Jenkins’s paper has been widely cited in the academic community. Despite the prevalence of the term, little analysis has been made into understanding the biases and implications of how it positions the relationship between player and environment.

Jenkins’s piece is a response to debates in the games community at the time over the correct way to theorize narrative in games (if at all). In his words, he “hope[s] to offer a middle ground position between the ludologists and the narratologists, one that respects the particularity of this emerging medium—examining games less as stories than as spaces ripe with narrative possibility.” Jenkins, instead of looking at plot arcs and cutscenes as the focus for his theorization of narrative in games, shifts the discussion to spaces that are ripe with the “possibility” of narrative. The game space, to Jenkins, “becomes a kind of information space, a memory palace,” a place that has information the player can extract and that reflects the actions of the player. A context in which boys can “struggle to explore, map, and master contested spaces.” It is a space where stories are “enacted” by the player and information is “embedded,” and in both of these constructions the environment is a background space dependent upon the player to have its narrative potential actualized.

*ANATOMY*, released by Kitty Horrorshow in 2016, initially presents a digital space that is similarly positioned as background. *ANATOMY* is a first person game in which the player navigates a dark house at night, collecting and playing cassette tapes that contain what appears to be a lecture on the “anatomy” of houses. At the beginning of the game, playing these tapes unlocks new parts of the house, and triggers a prompt directing the player to the location of other tapes. The game can be played multiple times, and will be different each time until its fifth incarnation. At the end of each playthrough, the game exits abruptly with no input from the player.

During these initial phases of the game, the relationship between player and space has a conventional structure that can be effectively read through the lens of environmental storytelling. One of Jenkins’ primary concepts of environmental storytelling is that of “embedded narratives”: narratives that are “embedded within the mise-en-scene awaiting discovery.” The house in *ANATOMY* fits this description well, as it is a passive background that contains information about the world, and the player uncovers more about the space they’re in as they explore it. This information takes the form of tapes, most obviously, but also in the furnishings (it’s a home from the 90s, given the TV) and decorations which are primarily anatomical illustrations (which re-emphasize the connection between body and home). The player is able to slowly but surely form a mental map of the house as they unlock more of its parts by collecting the tapes. Thus far in the game, the house is a space to be read, a repository of information to be mined.

In addition to being a space to be read, the house figures as a place where the player can enact their agency. The player can move about the space at will, and while certain doors are locked, they gradually become unlocked as the player collects tapes. The player is also able to exert other forms of control on the space, from breaking plates to turning on the television. This is a place where the player and their actions take center stage, where they are able to create spatial “enacted stories,” what Jenkins defines as “stories [that] are held together by broadly defined goals and conflicts and pushed forward by the character’s movement across the map.” The house, here, acts as backdrop, as the ground upon which actions take place, and its whole existence seems to be intended to facilitate the actions of the player.

While the concept of environmental storytelling may be an effective way to consider narratives in games, the conceptual structure it applies to player and space reflects and reinforces a problematic paradigm on the environment that has a long history.

Frankfurt School theorists Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer link the positioning of non-human nature as a passive resource to Enlightenment thought: “Enlightenment stands in the same relationship to things as the dictator to human beings. He knows them only to the extent that he can manipulate them…. Their ‘in-itself’ becomes ‘for him’” (6). They argue that this is part of the conceptual underpinning behind the exploitation of bodies both human and non-human, as “the control of internal and external nature has been made the absolute purpose of life” (24). Ecofeminist thinker Val Plumwood extends this critique, arguing that the domination and exploitation of human and non-human bodies that the figuring of nature as passive supports is gendered and racialized: “Racism, colonialism and sexism have drawn their conceptual strength from casting sexual, racial and ethnic difference as closer to the animal and the body construed as a sphere of inferiority, as a lesser form of humanity lacking the full measure of rationality or culture… whose domination is simply ‘natural’” (4). Jenkins problematically takes the idea that players would control “external nature” for granted, as he claims that “game designers have to develop worlds where we can touch, grab, and fling things about at will.” As Adorno, Horkheimer, and Plumwood take pains to show, positioning environments in this way has devastating consequences on all things conceptually linked to “nature.”

While *ANATOMY* initially seems to have a passive environment, over the course of the game it subverts this understanding of the digital space and, in doing so, even subverts stable, logocentric articulations of subjectivity. It accomplishes this in part by shaping the house as a space that is not so easily read, even in the early stages of the game. The last tape of the first playthrough is in the master bedroom, and rather than having to return to the kitchen to play it as they normally would, the player finds a cassette player in the bedroom floating on the wall. The impossibility of this arrangement is the first indication that the house is fluid and illogical. This carries over into the second playthrough, as the audio from the tapes is now garbled and the instructions text on where to go next is distorted. The stability of the space begins to break down, with furniture positioned in impossible ways and doors opening onto other doors. Images on the walls begin to flicker, and strange lines span diagonally across rooms. Tape players requiring no tapes appear and play different voices from what we have heard before. The house is no longer such an untroubled repository of information to be acted upon by the player.

In addition to becoming less easily intelligible, the house also begins to gain agency of its own, moving from being the game’s background to being a character, from being an object to a subject. The final tape in the first playthrough tells the player that, in sleep, “the bedroom seems less like a mind and more like a mouth…. It is here that we place ourselves most at the house’s mercy, and spend each night hoping that it won’t bite down.” The house, in this line, is figured not only as a body, but as a *living* body that can bite, that has hunger and thus desires. This becomes literal at the end of the second playthrough. The final tape begins as normal, the narrator describing the anatomy of homes, specifically the bedroom, but on the word “dreams,” there is a switch to a new voice. This voice is higher pitched, perhaps female, and speaks in the first person, apparently as the house itself. It recounts a dream in which “a young man… enters through the front door,” “urinates on the wall, spits on the carpet,” “breaking and upsetting things.” The dream concludes by saying that the house is “angry at him” and so kills him by slamming a door on his back, pushing him down the stairs to the basement. The basement door then opens while all other doors lock. When the player walks to the entrance of the basement, everything goes black, and the game ends once again. The player has thus been linked to the young man from the house’s dream, and their presence in the house has been recontextualized as an intrusion into a living being. The house has moved definitively from being a background upon which an agent acts to a character, a subject with desires, dreams, and a will of its own.

The subjectivity that the house acquires, however, is not a straightforward, logocentric subjectivity. It is not the kind of subject that is aligned with the male, the rational, the human; figurations that Plumwood claims form one side of a conceptual dichotomy that dominates the female, the irrational, and the non-human (43). The house is a non-human object become subject, one that is associated, through voice and domesticity, with femininity, one for which physicality and emotion are prominent. Furthermore, this subjectivity is definitively multiple and unstable, constituting what Dana Luciano and Mel Y. Chen argue is a queering of subjectivity, asserting that beings are not “rigid, bounded, and pure but flexible, multiple, and fluid, composed not only of different identities but different entities, different materialities” (186).

Over the course of the third playthrough, this becomes increasingly clear, as the house begins to form into a house of flesh, a body, and, in particular, an angry non-human body with multiple voices. The walls and furniture overlap and flicker while the walls of the bedroom look like the concrete of the basement. Fleshy sinews span across rooms and hallways. The house is an unstable, uncertain, changing body/space that is difficult to navigate, as the layout keeps shifting. This characterization of the fictional body/space of the house not only resists comprehension and manipulation at the hands of the player, it also resists narrow understandings of subjectivity that, as Luciano and Chen argue, are oppressive to de-humanized groups, in particular the colonized and the queer. Instead of having the house become a subject in the conventional sense, it, like the “queer/trans body… unsettle[s] the human as the norm; it generates other possibilities—multiple, cyborgian, spectral, transcorporeal, transmaterial—for living” (Luciano and Chen, 187).

This space/subject enacts its agency on the player at the conclusion of the third playthrough. As the player moves through the space, collecting tapes that are this time completely unintelligible when played, they finally play the final tape which ends on the barely discernible words “bite down.” With this, the player’s position “glitches” and they move some feet from where they were standing before. The door they came through to enter the kitchen has been replaced with a wall of flesh. Only one path is left available to them, the stairs down to the basement. Going down, they find the space vastly expanded and themselves trapped. After some time, the room turns red and a voice, seemingly the original narrator’s, chastises the player’s avatar and, it seems, the player themselves, for “vivisecting” the house. Far from being dead, the house is a living being, and the player’s attempts to explore, understand, and master the space have been harmful to it. Teeth begin to grow through the floor and the ceiling, and the game ends. The player’s control over the space has been subverted by the house as body and as subject, and they are consumed by it.

Subsequent playthroughs see the player nearly, if not completely, unable to act in a conventional game sense. At first, they are set outside the house, and unable to do anything but walk towards it. When they get close, they suddenly jump to being in the basement once more, this time seemingly lying on the floor, unable to move, with a cassette player before them. It plays of its own accord, and takes the voice of the second narrator that speaks about the abandonment and loneliness of old houses. When this monologue ends, the player can do nothing but stare forward. Should they restart the game, they will always find themselves back where they were left, on the floor, staring at the cassette player. The player has lost any semblance of dominance over the space, instead able only to do what the narrator chastised them for not doing before they were eaten: “Your purpose was to listen, and yet at every turn you have pried, you have prodded, and you have interfered.” The player failed to be with and learn from the house in a way that doesn’t involve its domination.

*ANATOMY* is a game that engages directly with how players relate to the digital spaces they inhabit. Appearing, at first, to be a space meant to be read, somewhere the player can “enact” stories and uncover “embedded” meaning, the house that is the principal character of *ANATOMY* becomes something very different over the course of multiple playthroughs. As the player’s agency is gradually stripped away, the house becomes a subject, gains a voice, becomes a living body. The subject it becomes subverts the typical characterization of subjects as stable, rational, male, human, instead queering this notion of subjectivity itself. It is an angry, emotional, non-human subject, and in presenting itself in this way, it destabilizes notions not only of how videogame spaces should be characterized but also how subjectivity should be considered. We end the game not with a human subject understanding and mastering a digital space, but with the two lying together, player and space, neither acting, neither intruding, both watching and listening.
